Entering adulthood with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in Slovakia does not just mean a daily struggle with inattention, time management, or inner restlessness. It also means a struggle with money.

While the state generally covers treatment for children in full or in part, newly diagnosed adult patients face a harsh reality: they must pay out of pocket for the ability to function normally, work, and concentrate. The amounts are so high that, when calculated by weight, they even exceed the price of precious metals.

### Mathematics That Makes No Sense

For comparison, one gram of pure gold trades at around **€120** on global commodity markets. For €50, you can therefore buy just under **half a gram of gold**.

However, if you look at the pharmacy receipt of an adult with ADHD, you will find that they pay **the same amount** for just a few milligrams of active ingredient in their tablets as they would for the aforementioned half a gram of gold.

While a 17-year-old student with the same diagnosis usually pays between **€0 and €4** out of pocket for their medication at the pharmacy, the situation changes dramatically upon turning 18 (or when receiving a diagnosis in adulthood):

* Livizux (lisdexamfetamine): approximately **€62 to €75** per month (depending on dosage)
* &#x20;Concerta / Methylphenidate (higher doses): approximately **€35 to €50** per month
* &#x20;Atomoxetine (non-stimulant treatment, e.g. Atofab, Strattera): approximately **€40 to €75** per month

For an adult, this means annual expenses of **€400 to €900** in medication co-payments alone. Why is this the case?

### The Trap of Slovakia's Drug Reimbursement Policy

This significant financial disparity is no coincidence. It is a direct consequence of how Slovakia's drug categorisation system is structured and of *Act No. 363/2011 Coll.*, which governs the scope and conditions for the reimbursement of medicines, medical devices, and dietary foods under public health insurance.

### An Outdated View of the Diagnosis

When ADHD medications were added to the categorisation lists of the Slovak Ministry of Health, they were approved primarily on the basis of **historical** clinical data focused on paediatric populations.

It was assumed that people would "grow out of" ADHD in adulthood. Although modern neuroscience and medicine established long ago that **the disorder persists throughout life in most people**, the state's reimbursement system has remained largely rooted in the past.

### The Age-18 Rule as an Impenetrable Barrier

Health insurance companies reimburse stimulant and non-stimulant medications for adult patients only if it can be demonstrated that they were already receiving treatment for ADHD **before turning 18** (the so-called indication restriction for continued treatment).

However, if you are diagnosed with ADHD at 20, 25, or even 30 — a common occurrence today thanks to greater awareness and improved diagnostics — the system automatically classifies you as a **self-paying patient**. The prescribing and indication restrictions in the Ministry of Health's categorisation list leave no room for exceptions: the insurance company will not contribute a single cent towards your medication, even though the treatment has been approved by the European Medicines Agency **(EMA)**.

### Budget Savings Instead of Prevention

The Categorisation Committee of the Ministry of Health assesses each medication in terms of financial efficiency (*the so-called cost-utility analysis under Act No. 363/2011 Coll.*). Providing universal reimbursement for modern treatment for tens of thousands of adult Slovaks would immediately generate millions of euros in additional expenditure for health insurance companies.

The state therefore prefers to transfer the entire financial burden directly onto patients.

However, this approach only appears economical and logical. In reality, it is an illusion created by the state budget.

Short-term savings on medication co-payments actually generate hidden costs many times higher in other areas of the healthcare and social welfare systems.

### Why Does Untreated ADHD Cost the State and Society More Than Treatment Itself?

1. Untreated ADHD in adulthood often leads to **secondary psychiatric diagnoses** (severe depression, anxiety disorders, and alcohol or medication dependence). Hospitalisation and long-term treatment for these conditions cost health insurance companies far more than ADHD pharmacotherapy itself.
2. Due to inattention and impulsivity, adults with untreated ADHD statistically have higher rates of road traffic accidents and injuries, placing a direct burden on emergency medical services and surgical care.
3. People without adequate treatment are more likely to experience burnout, lose their jobs, retire early on disability pensions, or contribute less in income tax. The state therefore loses productive workers and pays higher social benefits.
4. Impulsive behaviour and chronic stress contribute to higher rates of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and unhealthy lifestyles, resulting in costly long-term healthcare later in life.

Although the state saves money on its medication budget "here and now" by not covering medication co-payments for adults, it indirectly pays a **much higher price** in the long term through the treatment of resulting conditions, sick leave, disability pensions, and lost economic productivity.

### The Price of "Golden Pills"

For many people with ADHD, medication is not a luxury but a prerequisite for keeping a job, completing their education, or avoiding burnout and anxiety. However, the financial barrier forces many to interrupt treatment, reduce their doses without their doctor's approval, or abandon treatment altogether.

The result is an absurd paradox: the state saves a few dozen euros a month by not reimbursing a tablet, yet ultimately loses far more through reduced productivity, sick leave, and the treatment of secondary depression and anxiety. In Slovakia, adults with ADHD continue to pay the price of gold for a brain that functions.
