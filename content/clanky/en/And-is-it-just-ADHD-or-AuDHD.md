Have you ever felt that after years of searching for answers, you finally received an ADHD diagnosis, started taking medication or adjusting your routine, yet still felt like some pieces of the puzzle just didn't fit?
Perhaps ADHD medication helped tame your internal chaos and organize your thoughts, but suddenly you perceive surrounding noise much more intensely, social events drain you completely, or you feel anxious without a solid daily routine.

This is not necessarily a coincidence. More and more people are discovering that, alongside ADHD, they also manifest traits of autism. In both community and professional settings, the unofficial yet apt term AuDHD (Autism + ADHD) has become widely used for this combination.

In this article, we will look at what scientific research says, why this combination was overlooked for so long, how internal contradictions can manifest in AuDHD, and what you can do if you recognize yourself in some of these traits.

## Why wasn't AuDHD discussed earlier?

Just a few years ago, from a diagnostic standpoint, it was practically impossible to have both diagnoses at the same time. Until 2013, the **DSM-IV** diagnostic manual was in effect, which did not allow for the simultaneous diagnosis of ADHD and autism spectrum disorder.

It was assumed that symptoms such as inattention or hyperactivity could be part of autism and should not be diagnosed as separate ADHD. It was not until the introduction of the **DSM-5 in 2013** that it became possible to diagnose these two conditions simultaneously. Later, the **ICD-11** also introduced this possibility.

Today, therefore, ADHD and autism are considered two separate neurodevelopmental disorders that can co-occur in the same person.

## What do the statistics say?

Research shows that ADHD and autism significantly overlap in some people.

**How many people with autism also have ADHD?**

Research estimates that approximately **50–70% of people with autism** may also meet the criteria for ADHD. However, the specific figures vary across studies depending on the methodology used and the population studied.

**How many people with ADHD also have autism?**

Among people with ADHD, estimates of the prevalence of co-occurring autism range from approximately **20–50%**, although these figures vary significantly across studies.

These data indicate that ADHD and autism have significant clinical and genetic overlap. However, this does not mean that every person with ADHD also has autism, or vice versa.

## How might AuDHD manifest itself in practice?

Living with a combination of ADHD and autism can present specific challenges. This is because ADHD and autism can create different—and sometimes even conflicting—needs.

As a result, a person may find themselves caught in a constant tension between two distinct tendencies.

### 1. The Desire for Novelty Versus the Need for Routine

**ADHD Perspective:**  
May seek out change, spontaneity, new stimuli, and excitement. Routine can become boring over time.

**Autism side:**  
May seek predictability, stability, a fixed system, and familiar procedures.

**Result:**  
You create a daily routine that gives you a sense of stability, but after a few days, it starts to bore you, and your ADHD need for novelty leads you to disrupt it. As a result, you may feel stress or anxiety from the loss of control and predictability.

### 2. Sensation Seeking versus Sensory Overload

**ADHD side:**  
May seek out excitement, intense experiences, busy environments, or loud music.

**Autistic side:**  
May be markedly sensitive to light, sound, smells, touch, or clothing textures, and can become sensory overloaded quickly.

**Result:**  
You go to a party because you want to experience something new and fun, but after a short time the noise, lights, and crowd start to overwhelm you and you need to leave.

### 3. Social Contact versus Social Exhaustion

**ADHD side:**  
May be talkative, impulsive, sociable, and easily excited about conversation or meeting new people.

**Autistic side:**  
May bring difficulty interpreting certain social cues, increased difficulty with social situations, and a need for alone time to recover.

When trying to adapt one's behavior to the expectations of others, so-called **masking** can also occur – the conscious or automatic suppression or compensation of autistic traits.

## How to Recognize That It Might Be AuDHD?

Many adults only start considering autism after receiving an ADHD diagnosis. This is because ADHD can mask or make it harder to recognize certain autistic characteristics.

Possible signals include, for example:

- **Marked sensory sensitivity**, which you may become more aware of, for instance, after improving your attention or reducing your inner chaos.
- **Long-term intense interests**, which can be typical of autism, combined with short-term intense interests or "hyperfixations," which are often associated with ADHD.
- **Significant social exhaustion**, even after activities you also enjoy.
- **A feeling of being different or not fitting in**, which persists even though you understand the manifestations of ADHD and autism.
- **A need for routine and predictability**, which at the same time conflicts with the need for novelty and stimulation typical of ADHD.
- **Frequent alternation between hyperfocus, intense stimulation, and subsequent exhaustion.**

The mere presence of these traits, however, does not mean a person has AuDHD. Similar experiences can also occur in people with ADHD, anxiety disorders, depression, or other difficulties.

## Diagnosis by a Professional

Online tests, self-diagnosis, and screening questionnaires can serve as an orienting first step. On their own, however, they cannot confirm a diagnosis.

Diagnosis of ADHD and autism should be based on a comprehensive clinical assessment by a professional who takes into account developmental history, current presentation, functioning across various areas of life, and possible alternative explanations.

### 1. Detailed Anamnestic Interview and Developmental History

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition whose manifestations are present from early development. A professional may therefore ask about childhood, speech development, social relationships, play, school functioning, interests, repetitive behaviors, or reactions to sensory stimuli.

In diagnosing adults, an interview with a parent or another person who knew the individual in childhood can also be useful. In some cases, standardized tools such as the **ADI-R (Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised)** are used.

### 2. Standardized Diagnostic Methods

Professionals may use standardized tools to assess social communication and other autism characteristics, such as the **ADOS-2 (Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition)**.

However, the result of a single test does not determine a diagnosis on its own. These tools are part of a broader clinical assessment.

### 3. Differential Diagnosis

An important part of the evaluation is distinguishing which manifestations relate to ADHD, which to autism, and which may have another cause.

A professional may consider, for example, anxiety disorders, social anxiety, depression, trauma, or other psychological or neurodevelopmental difficulties.

### 4. Clinical Evaluation and Final Report

Diagnosis does not mean simply "checking boxes" on a questionnaire. It is a comprehensive clinical assessment of developmental history, current functioning, behavior, social communication, and the way stimuli are processed.

The result may be a professional report describing the findings and any recommendations for further support or treatment.

## What to Do If You Recognize Yourself in This?

If, while reading these lines, you feel a sense of relief and some things are starting to click into place, you can try a few practical steps:

1. **Get to know your needs:**  
   If you have ADHD and also notice autistic characteristics in yourself, it can be helpful to observe what helps you and what overwhelms you. You don't have to force yourself into a system that works for only one type of need.

2. **Adjust your strategies:**  
   If routine suits you, try setting it up flexibly enough. Instead of one strict morning routine, you could have, for example, three alternatives to choose from depending on your current energy and situation.

3. **Protect your senses:**  
   If you are easily overwhelmed by noise, light, or crowds, it can help to adjust your environment to your needs. Noise-canceling (ANC) headphones, dimmer lighting, or regular breaks from stimuli can all be useful.

4. **Consider a professional evaluation:**  
   If you have a long-standing feeling that an ADHD explanation alone doesn't cover your experience, you can seek out a psychologist or psychiatrist experienced in diagnosing ADHD and autism in adults.

5. **Seek understanding and support:**  
   Contact with people who have similar experiences can help you better understand your own functioning and reduce the feeling that you're alone with your experiences. Community, however, cannot replace a professional diagnosis.
