The way ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) is perceived in society is often limited to the stereotype of a restless child sitting in a school desk. The reality, however, is far more complex. Many people with this form of neurodivergence reach adulthood without ever realizing that their brain works differently.

Undiagnosed and untreated ADHD in adults comes with many silent struggles, from problems at work to difficulties in relationships. One of the most dangerous and common consequences associated with this hidden condition, however, is an increased vulnerability to addiction, particularly excessive alcohol consumption.

## Why Does Adult ADHD So Often Go Unnoticed?

In adulthood, the symptoms of ADHD can change. Physical hyperactivity often becomes less obvious and may instead turn into inner restlessness, a constant whirlwind of thoughts, and an inability to switch off and relax. Over the years, neurodivergent adults learn to mask their symptoms. They hide their disorganization, exhaustion from constantly having to concentrate, and the chronic feeling that they do not fit in.

This pressure can lead to exhaustion, anxiety and depression, which may obscure the underlying cause of the problem: untreated ADHD.

## The Search for Dopamine

To understand why alcohol can be so appealing to adults with ADHD, we need to look at the biology. The ADHD brain involves differences in neurotransmitter systems, particularly those involving dopamine and noradrenaline. These chemical messengers play important roles in regulating attention, motivation and reward.

While a typical brain can often gain enough satisfaction from ordinary everyday activities, a person with ADHD may seek stronger stimulation to activate the brain's reward system. This can lead to an unconscious search for intense experiences, extreme sports, impulsive behaviour, or the use of psychoactive substances.

## Alcohol as a Form of "Self-Medication"

For an adult with undiagnosed ADHD, alcohol may initially feel like a magical solution. This phenomenon is commonly referred to as self-medication. What does it look like in practice?

A person with ADHD may often feel as though ten televisions are playing in their head at the same time. Alcohol acts as a central nervous system depressant. After a few drinks, racing thoughts may slow down and the desired sense of silence can finally arrive.

People with ADHD may also experience social anxiety because they worry about impulsive reactions or interrupting others. Alcohol can temporarily reduce these inhibitions and feelings of anxiety.

Alcohol can also increase dopamine signalling in the brain, temporarily producing a stronger sense of reward and potentially reinforcing repeated use.

# From Relief to Addiction

What starts as an innocent "drink to relax after a hard day" can, in some people with ADHD, develop into problematic alcohol use and eventually addiction. The problem is that, in the long term, alcohol can significantly worsen ADHD-related difficulties.

Planning, organization and self-control may already be challenging for people with ADHD. Regular alcohol consumption, particularly the effects of a hangover the following day, can further impair these executive functions.

One of the characteristics associated with ADHD is impulsivity and difficulty with self-regulation. For someone with ADHD, it may therefore be particularly difficult to say "I've had enough" and go home after one drink.

The brain can also adapt to repeated exposure to alcohol's rewarding effects. Over time, a person may need to drink more or more frequently to achieve the same feeling of "switching off" their mind.

## The Way Out

Breaking this vicious cycle is extremely difficult without understanding the underlying problem. If only the alcohol addiction is treated while ADHD remains undiagnosed and unaddressed, the risk of relapse may remain significant. The brain may continue searching for ways to regulate distress, stimulation and reward.

So what steps can help?

For adults who recognize lifelong difficulties with attention, restlessness and impulsivity, it is important to seek assessment from a psychiatrist or psychologist experienced in working with neurodivergent adults.

Appropriate ADHD treatment, whether stimulant or non-stimulant medication, can improve attention, impulse control and other ADHD symptoms. Some adults report that after starting ADHD treatment, their desire to drink alcohol decreased because they no longer felt the same need to use alcohol as a form of self-medication. However, treatment response varies from person to person.

Cognitive behavioural therapy and a deeper understanding of how your own brain works can help build healthier coping mechanisms for stress that do not involve reaching for a bottle.

Admitting that you have a problem with addiction requires enormous courage. For many adults, however, discovering previously unrecognized ADHD can be the missing piece of the puzzle that finally helps them understand their behaviour and take greater control over their life and health.
