# On the Same Wavelength: Why ADHD and Autism Belong Together

Imagine spending your entire life listening to a radio filled with constant static. You can barely understand the presenter, the music plays to a strange rhythm, and you have to concentrate incredibly hard just to catch the point. Then, suddenly, you meet someone who switches to your frequency. The static disappears. Suddenly, everything makes sense.

This is exactly the feeling neurodivergent people — especially those with ADHD and those on the autism spectrum — can experience when they meet and start communicating with each other.

For years, society and medicine convinced us that these were two completely separate groups. Today, modern research shows that we have much more in common than it might seem. And most importantly? Our strength lies in understanding each other in a way that few others can.

But first, let's dispel an old myth. For a long time, it was believed that you could not have both ADHD and autism. The truth is that you can have both. Meet AuDHD:

Until 2013, psychiatric diagnostic practice treated ADHD and autism as mutually exclusive diagnoses. A person could be diagnosed with one or the other, but not both at the same time.

Today, we know that this was a mistake. Modern research shows a substantial overlap between the two conditions.

More than half of autistic people may also experience clinically significant symptoms of ADHD.

At the same time, many people with ADHD display autistic traits, such as sensory overload, a need for certain routines or deep, highly specific interests.

There is even an informal but widely used term for this combination: **AuDHD**. These two neurotypes can be thought of like siblings. Sometimes they clash — your ADHD side may crave spontaneous adventure while your autistic side panics because it disrupts a familiar and predictable plan — but ultimately, they come from the same family.

## The Science of Relationships: Why Do We Understand Each Other So Well?

The neurotypical world often makes us feel that we "don't know how to communicate", that we are awkward in relationships, too loud, too quiet, or simply "weird".

Research has, however, contributed to a concept known as the **Double Empathy Problem**. This concept suggests that communication difficulties between autistic and non-autistic people may be mutual rather than simply a deficit on the autistic person's side.

When a person with ADHD talks to a neurotypical person, misunderstandings can often occur. But when two neurodivergent people — for example, a person with ADHD and an autistic person — talk to each other, communication may suddenly feel much more natural.

Here are a few examples of what this can look like in practice:

**"Infodumping" is like our love language**

In the conventional world, talking for 40 minutes about how a nuclear reactor works or which build is best in an RPG game might be considered excessive. People with ADHD and autistic people, however, may express enthusiasm and connection in exactly this way. It is often called infodumping. When an autistic person or someone with ADHD passionately shares everything they know about a favourite topic, it can mean: "I feel safe with you and I want to share a part of myself with you." And we understand that. We listen enthusiastically, even when we know absolutely nothing about the subject.

**Shared solitude**

Remember when you were children and sat in a sandbox? You were building a castle while the child next to you was making sandcastles of their own. You were not talking or necessarily playing together, but you were there next to each other and it felt great.

Neurodivergent adults can love this kind of connection. One person can sit on the sofa wearing headphones and playing a console game, while the other sits in an armchair reading a book. Silence. No pressure to "entertain" the other person. Just the pure, safe feeling that we are not alone.

**Interrupting each other**

Neurotypical people may see interrupting as rude. For people with ADHD and some autistic people, however, it can sometimes be a sign that we are actively engaged in the conversation. We may interrupt because we immediately want to share a similar experience and say: "I understand you — I've experienced the same thing!"

Between us, it does not necessarily have to be an argument. It can be an energetic dance in which we naturally build on each other's thoughts and finish each other's sentences.

**Tolerance for "empty batteries"**

A neurodivergent person may be less likely to take it personally when you message them an hour before meeting:

*"Sorry, my social battery is completely drained today and everything is getting too much. I need to stay home."*

While another acquaintance might take it personally, an ADHD or autistic friend may simply reply:

*"I completely understand. Enjoy the quiet. We'll see each other when you've recharged."*

The world is largely designed around neurotypical ways of functioning. Schools, workplaces, institutions and social norms were built around systems that may not work equally well for everyone. This is one reason why everyday life can be so exhausting for neurodivergent people.

That is precisely why people with ADHD and people on the autism spectrum — and, of course, those who have both — can benefit from supporting one another. We are a community that does not need words to know when a room is simply too loud. We are people who will not judge you because you cannot open an email, cannot make eye contact or have chaos at home.

Autism can bring a strong attention to detail, deep loyalty and an ability to understand complex structures. ADHD can bring enormous energy, empathy and highly creative problem-solving.

When these two worlds come together — whether within one person through AuDHD or through friendships and relationships — the result does not have to be chaos. It can create a space where we can finally take off our masks and be ourselves.
