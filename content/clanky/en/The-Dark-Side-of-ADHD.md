## School: The First Wounds

For a child with ADHD, school can become a place where they repeatedly learn that they are not good enough. Not because they want to fail — quite the opposite. Many children with ADHD are above average or even gifted, yet the education system often evaluates them primarily on their ability to sit still for long periods, follow linear instruction and hand in homework on time. All three can be extremely difficult for an ADHD brain, not because of defiance, but because of differences in neurology.

The consequences are documented and recurring. A child receives disciplinary notes for disrupting the class. Parents are called to school. Teachers, often without any specific training in ADHD, may label the student as "lazy", "badly behaved" or "unmotivated". Classmates, who are highly sensitive to adults' social signals, quickly form their own perception. The child becomes a target of ridicule, is excluded from groups and may not be invited to birthday parties. The self-image formed during these years can follow that child for decades.

Research repeatedly shows that children with ADHD experience significantly higher rates of bullying than their peers. A study by Holmberg and Hjern (2008) found that up to 35% of ten-year-old boys with ADHD had been bullied by their classmates. It is not only physical harm that matters. Systematic social exclusion can be equally devastating — the kind where someone is not directly attacked, but simply seems not to exist to everyone else.

Research published in *PLOS One* documents higher rates of peer rejection among girls with ADHD across all studies examined, with effects ranging from moderate to large. More than half of children with ADHD have no close friend at school.

## Working Life: Invisible Obstacles

An ADHD diagnosis does not end at eighteen. The ADHD brain enters the workplace with many of the same challenges — only now there is no school bell, no scheduled recess and often less tolerance.

A typical day for an employee with ADHD can involve struggling with an open-plan office, a space almost designed to maximize distractions, endless meetings without clear structure, deadlines that they can plan but know their brain may simply fail to "see" when the critical moment arrives, and colleagues who interpret fluctuations in performance as disinterest or arrogance. The frustration goes both ways, but the system does not necessarily accommodate both sides equally.

According to research published in *PMC* (Fuermaier et al., 2021), people with ADHD have a substantially higher risk of voluntarily leaving employment and a higher risk of dismissal compared with the general population. They also earn statistically less and face greater employment instability. Ironically, this same group can demonstrate high levels of creativity, crisis-solving ability and hyperfocus. These are qualities many companies actively seek, yet they may fail to recognize them because they come packaged in a way that does not fit conventional HR templates.

A literature review published in 2024 in *SAGE Journals* confirms that employees with ADHD can thrive in the right environment, while systems based on rigid planning, inflexible deadlines and a lack of structured support may fail to accommodate their challenges.

## Emotional Dysregulation: The Story Nobody Talks About

If hyperactivity is the visible face of ADHD, emotional dysregulation is the invisible one — and perhaps one of the most disruptive. People with ADHD may experience emotions with greater intensity and have more difficulty regulating them. Joy can feel euphoric. Disappointment can feel devastating. Even constructive criticism can hurt deeply.

This phenomenon is sometimes referred to as **RSD, or Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria**. It is not simply being spoiled or dramatic. It describes an intense emotional response to perceived or actual rejection and criticism that is increasingly being studied in the context of ADHD. To someone on the outside, it may look like an overreaction. To the person experiencing it, it can feel like an emotional storm without a lifeline.

This dysregulation can also contribute to difficulties in relationships, both romantic and platonic. Partners and friends may not understand the intensity of the reactions. Relationships can break down. And every additional rejection can reinforce the belief that the person has been whispering to themselves since primary school: *Something is wrong with me.*

***

## Depression and Anxiety: A Predictable Consequence

Years of undiagnosed or unmanaged ADHD can leave lasting consequences. Clinical studies consistently show that adults with ADHD experience depression and anxiety disorders at substantially higher rates than the general population. This is not simply random — multiple factors associated with ADHD can contribute to this increased vulnerability.

A comprehensive review published in *Frontiers in Psychiatry* (2025) documents a high rate of comorbidity between ADHD and anxiety and depressive disorders. A Swedish population study involving more than 5.5 million adults found that depression and anxiety are among the most common psychiatric comorbidities in adults with ADHD. Research also suggests that stress, depression and anxiety can develop in the context of undiagnosed and untreated ADHD.

Imagine decades of trying your absolute hardest while your results repeatedly fail to match your effort. Missed deadlines despite working yourself to exhaustion. Damaged relationships despite caring deeply. Missed opportunities despite having talent. Each of these moments can become another layer of evidence supporting a core belief that formed in childhood: *I am not good enough. I will never be good enough.*

The hopelessness that can follow is not simply pessimism as a personality trait. It can be the exhaustion of having to fight against your own difficulties every day, meet standards designed around a different type of brain, and constantly explain to others why things are not easy for you.

***

## Suicide Risk: Numbers That Should Shake Society

This is the part that the media often avoids. It is uncomfortable and collides with a powerful social taboo. But that is precisely why it is important to talk about it directly.

People with ADHD have a statistically documented increased risk of suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts. A meta-analysis published in 2025 in *Brain and Behavior* (Garas et al.), based on longitudinal studies, confirmed that individuals with ADHD are at increased risk of suicidal behaviour. Another analysis estimates that ADHD is associated with approximately a threefold increase in suicide risk even after accounting for psychiatric comorbidities. One large study found that almost half of young people with ADHD had experienced suicidal thoughts, compared with approximately 6% of their peers without ADHD.

The key word is *untreated*. You can only receive appropriate treatment when you know what you are dealing with. And in Slovakia, thousands of people still do not know.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Years of cumulative failures. The feeling of being a burden to others. The belief that no matter what you do, it will never be enough. Exhaustion from living a life that seems to require three times the energy for half the results. Social isolation. Broken relationships. This is not an abstract risk profile — it describes the everyday reality of many adults with ADHD.

Appropriate diagnosis, treatment and, above all, understanding — both from the individual and from those around them — can help reduce these risks. Awareness can save lives. That is not merely a rhetorical statement; it is supported by evidence showing that identifying and addressing suicide risk and its underlying factors matters.

***

## Slovakia: A Country Where Nobody Talks About It

In Slovakia, there is no systematic nationwide public awareness campaign focused on ADHD in adults. Waiting times for psychiatric assessment can stretch into months. Many general practitioners have limited knowledge of ADHD beyond childhood hyperactivity. Teachers can go through their entire pedagogical training without mandatory education about neurodevelopmental disorders. Parents often learn about ADHD primarily through social media, which can be a valuable source of information, but cannot replace institutional support.

The consequences of this information gap are measurable: late diagnoses — particularly among women and girls — unnecessary years of suffering, interrupted careers, broken relationships and, in extreme cases, lost lives. Every year without systemic change is another year in which people are left searching for answers on their own, late at night, on a smartphone, convinced that something is wrong with the world and that they themselves are the flaw in the glass.

***

## What Could Help

**Education in primary schools.** Introduce basic education about neurodevelopmental disorders, including ADHD, dyslexia and autism, into teacher training and mandatory continuing education for active educators. A child sitting in a classroom with a teacher who understands ADHD has a fundamentally different experience from a child sitting with a teacher who sees them as a problem to be solved.

**Education for parents.** Develop systematic programs for parents, not only for those whose child has a diagnosis, but for everyone. An ADHD brain within a family can place a burden on the entire family system when nobody understands it. It can become a source of strength and individuality when the family understands what it is dealing with.

**Accessible diagnosis and treatment.** Reduce waiting times, increase the capacity of psychiatric care and, above all, reduce stigma. Create an environment in which seeking help is not seen as weakness, but as a responsible decision by an adult who wants to take care of their health.

**Media responsibility.** Slovak media also have a share of responsibility on this issue. Every article that reduces ADHD to a "fashionable diagnosis" or an "excuse for lazy people" can contribute to stigma and harm real people. Every interview that presents the full complexity of ADHD can help someone avoid years of unnecessary suffering.

***

## Conclusion

ADHD is not an invention, a trend or an excuse. It is a neurodevelopmental difference that, with appropriate support, can coexist with significant strengths, while a lack of support can contribute to serious and preventable difficulties. Slovakia has the knowledge, tools and people willing to help. What is needed is to move the topic from the margins of public discussion toward the centre of attention.

Those who are sitting in school classrooms today with an ADHD brain do not deserve another decade of silence.
